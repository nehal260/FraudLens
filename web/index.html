How it works

Five parts, the complete workflow

1

The data

Card transactions with a time, an amount and 28 scrambled columns, each already labelled normal or fraud.

2

The problem

Fraud is a tiny fraction. A lazy model that says "all normal" looks great on accuracy and catches nothing.

3

Training

80% of rows to train on, 20% held back for testing, split so both keep the same fraud share. Fraud examples are weighted up.

4

Comparing

Logistic Regression, Gradient Boosting and Random Forest, all evaluated on the same held-out test set.

5

The app

The winning forest is exported and runs in this page: pick a transaction, get a score and a verdict.

Limitations to keep in mind
  • The public data covers a short window of European card payments, so patterns may not transfer to other regions or years.
  • V1 to V28 are PCA-scrambled, so a flag cannot be explained in business terms. The "why this score" view shows which scrambled columns mattered, not what they mean.
  • Results are on one train/test split. A production system would use time-based validation and monitor drift.
  • Fraud tactics change. The model would need regular retraining and human review of its flags.

Looks brilliant, catches nothing. That is why FraudLens is judged on precision and recall instead.

The 28 scrambled columns

V1 to V28 were produced by a mathematical scrambling (PCA) before the data was published, so nobody can read the original card details. The model can still learn patterns in them. It just can't tell us in words what each one means.
